Approach

What the app is for, what I've decided not to build, what's next, and how I look after it.

What it's for

Deciding what to cook tonight from what's already in. I mostly open it just before cooking, so most decisions come back to that: it should give an answer quickly, without much input.

That needs three things. Stock has to be quick enough to keep up to date that it stays accurate. Recipes have to be structured enough that the app can check them against stock. And generated recipes have to be good enough to actually cook.

Not planned

Stock quantities and low-stock alerts

Once stock holds numbers, it needs updating every time you cook, and within a couple of weeks it's wrong, at which point the suggestions are wrong too. In or out is less precise, but it's something I still keep up to date.

Shopping lists, or “what am I missing?”

The app is for cooking from what you have. A recipe you can't make tonight doesn't get cooked tonight, and tracking what's missing would pull the app back towards being an inventory.

Meal planning

Planning the week is a different job, done at a different time. This is for the decision at six o'clock.

Nutrition and calories

A language model's nutrition figures range from rough to wrong, and precise-looking numbers get trusted. It's not worth the risk, and it isn't what the app is for.

Star ratings

I built them and took them out before release. Nothing would have used them: they can't go into the brief without steering it towards what we already ate, favourites already mark the good ones, and sorting two people's recipes by average score isn't useful. Notes cover it better, because “needed ten minutes longer, less chilli” is the part worth reading again.

General cooking chat

@kitchen answers questions about the recipe you're looking at, where the context is already there. An open-ended cooking chat isn't planned; other apps do that well.

Sharing between households

Anything public turns a private tool into a platform, with accounts, moderation and privacy exposure. Sending someone a recipe is covered by the print and share view, which gives you a file rather than access to your instance.

AI-generated food photos

The picture wouldn't be of the dish you're about to make, so at best it's generic and at worst it's misleading.

This is how I see it now. A couple of these have changed before, and this list is mostly here so I don't have to work the same reasoning out again.

Roadmap

  • Importing recipes from the web. Most recipe sites publish the recipe as structured data (schema.org) that can be read straight into the app's format, often with no AI at all. Typing recipes in is the main thing that slows down building up a collection, so this comes first.
  • Importing from a photo, for cookbooks.
  • A local model option, so generation can stay on your network too.

Roughly in that order, with no dates. What's actually shipped is on Releases.

Maintenance

This is a personal project that we use every day at home, shared in case it's useful to someone else. I fix what breaks and work through the roadmap; I'm not trying to grow the feature list. I'd rather have a small tool that still works in three years.

Bug reports and questions are welcome on GitHub. I read feature requests too, but the roadmap is short, and “no, and here's why” is a normal answer.
