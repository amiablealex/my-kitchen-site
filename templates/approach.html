Approach

What it does, and what it won't

My Kitchen is a deliberately small and light-weight tool with one job. Most of the decisions that shaped it were decisions not to build something. Here they are, with the reasons.

The one job

Turn what's actually in your kitchen into something worth cooking tonight. Every feature is judged against that moment: standing in the kitchen, hungry, wanting an answer rather than a project. It was assumed the app would rarely get used outside of immediately before intending to cook something.

Three things follow. Stock has to be effortless enough that it stays accurate. Recipes have to be structured and standardised enough that “can I make this?” is answerable. And any generated outputs have to be high quality.

Left out on purpose

Stock quantities, decrementing and low-stock alerts

The hardest guardrail in the project. The moment stock holds numbers, it needs maintaining every time you cook, and within a fortnight it's wrong — at which point the whole app is lying to you. In-or-out is coarse, but it's a toggle you'll still be updating in a year.

A shopping list, or “what am I missing?”

The app is reached for inspiration from what you have. A recipe you can't make tonight doesn't get cooked tonight, so the near-misses were never the interesting part. It'd also drag the app back towards inventory.

Meal planning and calendars

Different tool, different mindset. This one is for the decision you make at 6pm, not the one you make on Sunday.

Nutrition and calorie tracking

A language model's nutrition figures range from rough to confidently wrong, and numbers that look precise get trusted. Not worth the risk, and not what the app is for.

Star ratings

Built, then dropped before shipping. A rating is only worth storing if something consumes it — and nothing would. It can't feed the recipe generator without leading it towards last month's food; favourites already answer “which are the good ones”; and sorting a two-person recipe bank by average score is a report nobody runs. Notes cover the same ground better, because “needed 10 minutes longer, use less chilli” is the part worth re-reading.

A chat assistant

Open-ended conversation about cooking is well served by the apps built for it. The narrow version — asking a question about the recipe already on screen, where the context is already loaded — is worth having, and is on the list.

Sharing between households, and any public route

A community feature turns a private tool into a platform: moderation, accounts, scale, privacy exposure. The actual need — sending someone a recipe — is met by the print/share view, which hands you a PDF rather than opening a door into your instance.

AI-generated food photography

Generic at best and misleading at worst, since the picture won't be of the dish you're about to make.

None of this is a promise never to change my mind — a couple of these have been revisited already. It's a record of the reasoning, so the same ground doesn't get re-covered from scratch every time.

What's next

A short list, worked through slowly:

  • Importing recipes from the web. Most recipe sites publish structured data that can be read directly into the app's format — often with no AI involved at all. This is the big one, because typing recipes in by hand is the main friction in building up a bank.
  • Asking a question about a recipe. A box on the recipe page: swaps, substitutions, why a step works. Answers only — not rewriting the recipe by conversation.
  • Importing from a photograph, for cookbooks.
  • A local model option, so generation could stay on your network too.

In roughly that order, with no dates attached. See Releases for what's actually landed.

How this is maintained

It's a personal household project, used daily in my own kitchen and shared in case it's useful to someone else. That's the honest scope: I fix what breaks, I finish what's on the list above, and I'm not chasing a feature set. A small tool that still works in three years is the goal — not a big one that needs constant attention.

Bugs and questions are genuinely welcome on GitHub. Feature requests are read too, but the list above is short on purpose, and “no, and here's why” is a normal answer here.
